## Referential Continuity, Conceptual Evolution and Network Structure

**Research report — Observation snapshot: 30 October 2026**

## Abstract

Digital Identity Optimization (DIO) and the Ontology of Digital Identity (ODI), developed by Daniel Beránek, constitute a developing conceptual framework distributed across multilingual publications, persistent archival records, a machine-readable reference vocabulary, software repositories, application mappings and identity-linked platforms.

This study examines the implementation and distribution of DIO/ODI through its own conceptual perspective, complemented by heterogeneous graph analysis. Its central question is whether the distributed ecosystem sustains a recognizable, referentially continuous and meaningfully reconstructable identity across different representations and entry points despite partial inconsistencies, historical differences, incomplete connections and uneven updating.

The investigation begins with the machine-readable Reference Vocabulary and its source repository, then follows the relationships into the publication corpus, website, multilingual manifestations and secondary distribution layers.

The findings indicate that the ecosystem exhibits substantial but uneven reconstruction resilience. Its principal strengths are stable concept identifiers, explicit conceptual provenance, archival continuity, differentiated distribution roles and an increasingly structured metadata registry. Its principal weaknesses concern temporal alignment, historically changing semantic relations, incomplete publication navigation and differences between the document structures intended by an artifact and those exposed by particular platform interfaces.

A graph analysis of the repository's directed inter-work relation registry identifies 21 typed relationships among 17 distinct identifiers. The resulting network has a recognizable publication lineage and a strongly connected metadata aggregation layer, but these measurements describe the declared corpus relations rather than the global hyperlink network.

The study distinguishes conceptual continuity from representational uniformity and local acquisition difficulty from system-wide reconstruction failure. It concludes that, within the inspected observation domain, DIO/ODI remains meaningfully reconstructable, especially at the level of framework identity and principal conceptual relationships. Reconstruction of the latest publication state, individual language projections and particular distribution relationships is less uniform.

**Keywords:** Digital Identity Optimization; Ontology of Digital Identity; DIO; ODI; semantic coherence; referential continuity; identity-preserving variation; semantic vulnerability; knowledge representation; SKOS; graph theory; multilingual digital identity; provenance; entity resolution.

# 1. Introduction

Digital Identity Optimization (DIO) and the Ontology of Digital Identity (ODI) address a problem that becomes increasingly significant as digital identity is represented, interpreted and reconstructed across heterogeneous information environments.

An entity's digital identity is not contained in a single page, publication, database record or profile. It emerges through the relationships between distributed representations, their interpretations, the contexts in which they are encountered and the mechanisms through which observers reconstruct the underlying referent.

The DIO/ODI project applies this perspective to digital identity as both a theoretical subject and a field of deliberate optimization.

DIO represents the applied discipline concerned with improving the conditions under which an entity's digital identity can be reconstructed by relevant human and machine observers. ODI provides the theoretical meta-layer concerned with the existence, persistence, variation and reconstruction of digital identity itself. The distinction is explicit in the foundational manifesto and remains present in the current Reference Vocabulary.[1][2][3]

The project is itself distributed across a heterogeneous environment. Its representations include:

- A Czech-language foundational manifesto and fifteen additional language projections.
- A living Czech and English web presentation.
- Archival publications on Zenodo.
- Conceptual and analytical papers.
- A machine-readable SKOS/RDF vocabulary.
- JSON-LD and Turtle serializations.
- A versioned GitHub reference-model repository.
- Derived application mappings.
- Secondary distribution through Hugging Face, Kaggle and ResearchGate.
- Author-linked identifiers and AI-oriented context resources.

This creates an opportunity for a reflexive investigation: the framework's own conceptual resources can be used to examine the conditions under which its distributed representations preserve the identity of the framework and its author.

The relevant question is not whether every representation is complete, perfectly synchronized or identical. Nor is it whether every individual access route succeeds.

The question is whether the distributed ecosystem maintains sufficient referential and semantic continuity for an observer to reconstruct DIO/ODI meaningfully, despite differences between representations and the changing state of the underlying corpus.

The investigation therefore focuses on the relationship between three aspects:

1. **Conceptual identity:** whether the underlying framework and its principal distinctions remain recognizable.
2. **Referential continuity:** whether publications, authorship assertions, concepts and versions remain attributable to their intended referents.
3. **Reconstructability:** whether an observer entering through different parts of the ecosystem can recover a sufficiently compatible understanding of the framework.

These aspects are interdependent but not interchangeable.

A stable DOI can preserve the identity of a historical publication without guaranteeing that it describes the current conceptual state. A current vocabulary can clarify present terminology without explaining the historical development of every term. A multilingual archive can preserve broad conceptual correspondence without demonstrating equivalent currency across every language.

The significance of each condition must therefore be assessed relative to the reconstruction task it affects.

## 1.1 Observation boundary and temporal scope

The observation snapshot is 30 October 2026.

DIO/ODI is treated as an ongoing optimization and conceptual-development process rather than a permanently achieved state. The ecosystem is examined as it exists at a particular point in that process.

The investigation follows the sequence:

1. Machine-readable vocabulary and reference repository.
2. Archival publication and version infrastructure.
3. Website and professional-biographical representations.
4. Multilingual distribution.
5. Secondary distribution and machine-oriented resources.
6. Cross-layer relationships and graph structure.

Four analytical levels are distinguished throughout:

- **Artifact condition:** what is actually documented, present, missing or inconsistent.
- **Architectural role:** how the element contributes to or complicates DIO/ODI reconstruction.
- **Local consequence:** what a particular observer can recover or misunderstand through a particular pathway.
- **System-level significance:** whether the condition materially compromises reconstruction of the framework as a whole.

This distinction is essential because the presence of a reconstruction problem does not automatically establish identity collapse, just as the presence of a safeguard does not automatically establish successful protection.

# 3. Conceptual Architecture and Historical Development

## 3.1 DIO and ODI as related but differentiated levels

The central conceptual distinction is relatively stable across the examined representations.

DIO concerns the intentional optimization of conditions under which digital identity is reconstructed.

ODI concerns digital identity as an object of theoretical examination, including its existence, persistence, variation and interpretation.

The Reference Vocabulary formalizes the distinction:

- DIO is an applied discipline.
- ODI is its theoretical meta-layer.

Their relationship is represented through `skos:related`, rather than through a hierarchy that would imply one is simply a narrower instance of the other.[4]

The English manifesto expands this distinction by treating the two levels as different abstractions of the same broader problem.[3]

This distinction is consequential for reconstruction.

An observer who encounters only the name Digital Identity Optimization might classify the framework as a marketing methodology. An observer who also encounters ODI, the manifesto's theoretical sections and the reference vocabulary can recover a substantially broader conceptual ambition.

The two names therefore perform different but complementary identifying functions.

DIO communicates applied intent. ODI communicates the theoretical scope.

## 3.2 The K6 Semantic Connectome

The manifesto presents six mutually conditioned dimensions:

1. Entity.
2. Representation.
3. Interpretation.
4. Trust.
5. Relationship.
6. Reconstruction.

The K6 Semantic Connectome is explicitly represented as a complete graph rather than a linear process.

The dimensions are not sequential workflow stages. They describe mutually influencing aspects of digital identity within a shared semantic field.[3]

The vocabulary preserves K6 and identifies it as a finer-grained candidate model. It does not represent it as a logical consequence of MVDIO or as a literal causal graph.[4][9]

This qualification is important.

K6 is the framework's conceptual model of identity interdependence. It is not a description of how the publications, websites and archival records connect.

A mathematical representation of K6 as an ordinary undirected complete graph would contain six vertices and fifteen pairwise edges. Those conceptual relationships must not be confused with the actual hyperlinks or publication relations measured elsewhere in this study.

The manifesto also introduces the Semantic Field as a working analogy rather than a literal physical field.

The current vocabulary makes this distinction explicit: the Semantic Field is a modeled designation for the shared configuration of mutually conditioned dimensions, not an ontological substance.[4]

This is a useful example of conceptual development that retains expressive richness while introducing more precise interpretive constraints.

## 3.3 Minimum Viable DIO and the emergence of reconstruction conditions

The MVDIO paper, published on 8 October 2026, introduces a coarser analytical model developed through adversarial reduction of the broader DIO framework.[7]

It distinguishes three conditions that are easily conflated:

- The existence of a reconstruction mechanism.
- The existence of an optimizable digital identity problem.
- The deliberate intervention undertaken to address that problem.

Its basic analytical structure separates:

- A referentially continuous entity.
- Its distributed digital representational state.
- An observer and the reconstruction mechanism through which identity is inferred.

This reduction is important for the interpretation of the entire corpus.

It distinguishes an existing identity from the representations through which it is encountered and from the reconstruction performed by an observer.

It also introduces important qualifications:

- Consistency does not require uniformity.
- Divergent reconstructions are not automatically semantic fragmentation.
- Optimization requires a defined observation domain.
- Legitimate variation must remain anchored to referential continuity.
- Semantic vulnerability is a property of robustness and risk, not itself a realized failure state.

These distinctions help explain why the multilingual corpus and historical versions should not be evaluated against an ideal of textual uniformity.

A representation can differ from another without necessarily creating a different referent.

The relevant question is whether the difference preserves sufficient conceptual correspondence for the intended reconstruction task.

## 3.4 Reconstruction Failure Modes

The subsequent taxonomic note and Reference Vocabulary organize the failure taxonomy into four principal branches.[8][4]

| Category | Included concepts |
|---|---|
| Referential Reconstruction Failures | Entity Substitution, Merge Failure, Split Failure, Entity Omission |
| Semantic Coherence Failures | Semantic Fragmentation |
| Temporal Reconstruction Failures | Temporal Discontinuity, Temporal Misalignment |
| Contextual Adequacy Failures | Contextual Displacement |

Several related concepts remain outside the hierarchy:

- Identity Collapse is treated as a phenomenon-level concept.
- Semantic Vulnerability is a robustness-related property.
- Semantic Robustness is its conceptual counterpart.
- Reconstruction Capture remains related without a stronger causal classification.

This separation is analytically useful.

Not every weakness in the distributed corpus constitutes one of the taxonomy's failure modes.

A stale publication date, for example, can create temporal misalignment for a publication-status reconstruction task. It does not automatically establish semantic fragmentation or identity collapse of the framework itself.

Likewise, an inaccessible web viewer can obscure the relationships between files without demonstrating that the underlying documents have disappeared.

The taxonomy is most informative when applied at the level appropriate to the actual problem.

## 3.5 Identity-preserving variation and continuity

The vocabulary defines Referential Continuity as the condition under which an entity can change over time while remaining the same referent for analytical purposes.

Identity-Preserving Variation describes legitimate contextual differences between reconstructions that do not require uniformity.[4]

This distinction provides a particularly useful interpretive resource for the present investigation.

The DIO/ODI ecosystem contains:

- Historical publications.
- Living web documents.
- Multilingual projections.
- Updated conceptual definitions.
- Versioned archival artifacts.
- Derived technical mappings.

These are not expected to remain textually identical.

Their identity is sustained through the relationships that allow observers to recognize their common conceptual referent while distinguishing their specific historical or contextual states.

The vocabulary's treatment of Identity-Preserving Constraints further qualifies this relationship.

The current plural concept describes a domain-configured set of conditions. It does not imply one universal, context-free inventory of constraints.

The older singular URI is retained as a separate historical entry, with an editorial note explaining its status. No semantic equivalence between the two identifiers is asserted.

This is a controlled historical distinction rather than a forced merger.

It nevertheless introduces a cost for automated retrieval and entity resolution, because a machine cannot safely infer equivalence merely from similar labels.

These transitions contribute to continuity because historical changes remain traceable.

They also mean that an observer entering through the earliest manifesto will not reconstruct precisely the same level of detail as an observer entering through the current reference model.

That difference is a characteristic of the framework's development, not automatically a contradiction.

# 4. Distributed Representation and Cross-Layer Continuity

## 4.1 Machine-readable reference infrastructure

The Reference Vocabulary is the most explicit semantic anchor in the ecosystem.

Its canonical namespace is:

`https://vocab.danielberanek.cz/dio-odi/#`

The vocabulary provides stable concept identifiers, labels, definitions, broader/narrower relations, related-concept relations, source references and revision notes.

It is intentionally SKOS-first, with a possible future OWL layer reserved for stronger axioms justified by subsequent development and review.[4][10]

The practical significance of this decision is that the current model does not need to overstate its formal commitments in order to support machine-readable interpretation.

The vocabulary distinguishes:

- Concepts and their labels.
- Hierarchical and non-hierarchical relationships.
- Historical entries and current terminology.
- Canonical concepts and derived application mappings.
- Concepts and the publications from which they originate.

The current vocabulary's serializations include Turtle/SKOS/RDF and JSON-LD.

The repository documentation also identifies machine-query examples, a human-readable terminology file and a concept-to-source map.[10]

These provide complementary access methods.

A human reader can consult the terminology and source map. A semantic-web application can use the RDF representation. A system using JSON-LD can consume a structured serialization.

The same concept identifiers provide a common referential basis across these different modes.

### The importance of the source map

The `docs/source-map.md` file is particularly relevant to reconstructability.

It records the primary publication associated with each concept or concept cluster, together with a current-status interpretation.

This includes the current and historical treatment of Identity-Preserving Constraint(s), the movement of Semantic Vulnerability outside the failure hierarchy, and the refinement of Entity Substitution and Entity Omission.[9]

The source map is therefore more than a bibliography.

It documents the relationship between a concept's current representation and its historical development.

Its function can be interpreted within DIO as support for referential continuity and protection against semantic displacement.

It does not guarantee that every external observer will follow or correctly interpret the source relations, but it provides a concrete reconstruction path.

## 4.2 Repository structure and versioned releases

The GitHub repository is not simply a mirror of the vocabulary page.

It contains the reference model's source material, application mappings, metadata registries, documentation and citation metadata.

The repository's current archived release is v0.5.0, corresponding to Zenodo DOI `10.5281/zenodo.23643845`.

Its included vocabulary version is v0.2.0.

The preceding v0.4.0 archive contains vocabulary v0.1.0. Earlier releases include v0.3.2 and v0.3.1.[10][11]

This helps explain apparent differences between earlier descriptions of the repository and the later archived records.

The vocabulary version must be interpreted relative to the Reference Model release in which it is included.

A repository snapshot from one release and a later archive are not necessarily describing the same semantic state.

The current documentation explicitly identifies these release relationships, making it possible to reconstruct the progression rather than treating the differences as an unexplained inconsistency.

### The distinction between package and namespace

The repository also distinguishes the versioned archived software package from the persistent vocabulary namespace.

The archive represents a particular release of the Reference Model.

The namespace is intended to remain stable independently of the repository host.

The metadata relation between the two is deliberately held for semantic review rather than automatically asserted as `isIdenticalTo`.

This is an important instance of conservative identity assertion.

The package and the namespace are related representations, but the system does not automatically claim that they are identical entities.

That distinction is particularly relevant for automated publication and semantic-web applications.

## 4.3 Operational metadata and publication genealogy

The current metadata registry includes:

- `works.json`
- `relations.json`
- `distributions.json`
- `references.json`
- `keywords.json`

The registry explicitly describes itself as an operational metadata layer separate from the canonical vocabulary.[13][14][15]

This separation means that publication metadata can change without automatically changing concept definitions.

It also provides a way to distinguish different types of publication relationships.

For example:

- `isDerivedFrom` identifies conceptual or publication genealogy.
- `references` records citation relationships.
- `isNewVersionOf` identifies a later representation of a work.
- `isVersionOf` distinguishes version relations.
- `isIdenticalTo` identifies equivalent distribution copies.
- `isDocumentedBy` links the reference model to its website documentation.

This vocabulary of relations is more useful than a generic collection of hyperlinks because it conveys the intended nature of each relationship.

It also allows the registry to preserve distinctions between current and historical publication states.

### Version-scoped distribution relations

The registry provides a particularly instructive example involving the DIO Conceptual Framework.

Its original Zenodo record, DOI `10.5281/zenodo.21839423`, remains a valid historical publication.

The current version 1.1.0 is archived under DOI `10.5281/zenodo.23305929`, published on 11 October 2026.

The current record identifies corresponding ResearchGate, SSRN and Figshare representations as versions of the current work.

The registry explicitly warns against projecting the current relationship retroactively onto the earlier Zenodo version.[14][16]

This is a practical application of temporal reconstruction.

A relationship between two documents can change meaning when the version of the underlying work changes.

The registry preserves that distinction instead of forcing all historical representations into one timeless equivalence claim.

## 4.7 Hugging Face and Kaggle

The Hugging Face dataset is explicitly described as a secondary distribution layer for the canonical multilingual archive.

This number is not the number of manifesto documents.

The dataset card explains that the conversion process produces a unified text stream from the repository's HTML files and supporting text documents. The viewer does not expose the sixteen HTML files as sixteen separate file-level documents.

It explicitly recommends using the individual files in the Files and Versions interface when document-level structure matters.

This is a significant example of observer-relative reconstruction.

The source documents remain present according to the dataset's file inventory, but the default viewing mechanism does not faithfully expose their original document structure.

An observer relying only on the unified stream may lose language boundaries and document-level distinctions.

An observer retrieving the individual HTML files can recover them.

The same dataset card identifies a dataset size of approximately 8.14 MB and declares CC BY 4.0 for that distribution layer.

The Zenodo multilingual package is approximately 8.1 MB.[1][17]

The website and archival documentation also describe a Kaggle layer containing sixteen multilingual HTML documents and one hub document, designed for interactive browsing, search and structural comparison.

The Kaggle resource's role is documented, but its complete current interface and functionality were not independently inspected in this observation.

The important distinction is therefore between an intended distribution capability and a demonstrated outcome.

Hugging Face provides a directly inspectable secondary distribution. Kaggle is documented as another distribution pathway whose current behaviour requires separate verification.

### K6 conceptual graph

K6 represents six mutually conditioned dimensions of identity.

Its structure concerns conceptual interdependence, not publication navigation.

### SKOS vocabulary graph

The Reference Vocabulary represents hierarchical and related conceptual relationships.

It includes a taxonomy of Reconstruction Failure Modes and related concepts that deliberately remain outside that taxonomy.

### Publication and provenance graph

The metadata registry represents publications, source relationships, version relationships and distribution representations.

This is the graph for which reliable degree calculations are available.

### Website navigation and distribution graph

The website hub links to language versions, archival records, dataset representations, secondary publications and reference resources.

The full hyperlink graph has not been exhaustively crawled and measured. Its global in-degree, out-degree and centrality therefore cannot be established from the available evidence.

### 6.3.1 Translation misalignment

This is a meaningful weakness for observers who require the latest conceptual detail in every language.

It is less important for identifying the framework and its foundational relationships.

The Czech and English living editions provide access to later developments, while the archived multilingual edition preserves the earlier state in all sixteen languages.

The two representations do not provide equal temporal detail, but they retain inspectable historical relationships.

### 6.3.2 Distribution relation discrepancies

The MVDIO record illustrates a more technical problem.

The Zenodo metadata uses `IsSupplementedBy` for certain external copies. The current distribution registry records the author's verification that these resources contain the same research output and should instead be classified as `IsIdenticalTo`.[14]

The registry provides an inspectable correction, but it does not automatically modify historical Zenodo metadata.

This means that an automated observer entering through Zenodo may encounter a weaker relationship assertion than one entering through the current registry.

The problem concerns the semantics of distribution equivalence.

It does not establish that the MVDIO publication itself has been replaced or merged with another work.

### 6.3.3 Stale publication-status information

The Zenodo v0.5.0 Reference Model record contains a Notes statement indicating that GitHub release and Zenodo archival are pending.

However, the same record identifies version 0.5.0 as published on 25 October 2026 and provides the archived package.

The Notes field therefore gives an outdated indication of the publication process.[11]

An observer relying on that field may infer an incomplete archival state.

The publication record and downloadable package provide sufficient contradictory information to recover the actual published state.

This is a contained temporal inconsistency, but one that can impair automated interpretation.

### 6.3.4 Document-boundary loss in Hugging Face

The Hugging Face viewer exposes a technical preview in a form that does not preserve the original file-level structure of the multilingual manifesto.

The dataset card explicitly documents the limitation and directs readers toward the individual files.

This makes the reconstruction problem particularly clear:

- The source documents are present.
- The interface does not expose them in the intended structure.
- An alternative retrieval route is documented.
- The intended document-level representation remains recoverable.

This is a local observer-path problem, rather than evidence that the underlying multilingual corpus is incomplete.

### 6.3.5 Provenance, licensing and trust

The ecosystem uses DOI identifiers, author metadata, version histories, repository citations and source references to support attribution and provenance.

These mechanisms should not be confused with digital signatures.

A DOI identifies and resolves an archival object. Provenance metadata records relationships and origins. A digital signature would provide a different type of cryptographic assertion.

No universal cryptographic signing infrastructure was established in this investigation.

Similarly, CC BY 4.0 is explicitly documented for the manifest's secondary distribution, the Reference Model repository documentation and several individual publications.

The Hugging Face record includes a licence file, and the repository's licence and citation metadata identify CC BY 4.0.

This does not establish that every external artifact, file, illustration or third-party resource has an identical licence.

Licensing is therefore best reconstructed at the individual artifact level.

Zenodo and GitHub provide useful persistence and provenance functions, but their institutional or platform status does not establish the correctness of every conceptual claim or publication relation.

The manuscript records retain their actual publication types, including preprints and software. Their archival status must not be mistaken for evidence of peer-reviewed validation.

# 7. Application Mappings and Emergent Findings

## 7.1 DIO as a developing application architecture

The application mappings represent an important transition from conceptual definitions toward domain-specific use.

They do not redefine the canonical vocabulary.

Three mappings are particularly relevant.

### RAG Pipeline Mapping

The RAG mapping projects DIO Reconstruction Failure Modes onto pipeline loci, mechanisms, observable symptoms and hypothetical incidents.

Its later extension examines iterative and hypothesis-conditioned evidence acquisition.

This adds a process-oriented perspective: an agent's working hypothesis can influence the queries and evidence sources it chooses, which can subsequently shape the observations available to support that hypothesis.[10][11]

The extension does not establish that such feedback inevitably creates a reconstruction failure.

It identifies a potential relationship between evidence acquisition and subsequent reconstruction.

### HEM × DIO Mapping

The mapping between the Heterogeneous Entity Matching taxonomy and DIO reconstruction consequences applies external categories of heterogeneity to the analysis of possible reconstruction failures and Semantic Vulnerability.

It was introduced in Reference Model v0.3.0 and retained in later archived releases.

Its importance is that it connects DIO to an established technical problem domain without silently modifying the original DIO taxonomy.[10][11]

### Commit-Boundary × Policy-as-Code Mapping

This mapping connects DIO/ODI commit-boundary obligations with Policy-as-Code mechanisms, using OPA/Rego and Cedar as reference points.

It documents relevant capabilities and integration gaps.

The mapping is explicitly non-normative and non-deterministic. It does not claim a completed or empirically validated implementation.[11]

## 7.2 Constraining the Commit Boundary

The paper *Constraining the Commit Boundary: A DIO/ODI-Informed Authorization Guardrail for Agentic Entity Resolution*, published on 22 October 2026, provides a particularly significant application of the framework.[16]

It distinguishes evidence available to an agent for reasoning from evidence eligible to authorize persistent identity-changing mutations.

This distinction connects DIO/ODI reconstruction concepts to a concrete technical boundary.

The proposed architecture uses domain-configured Identity-Preserving Constraints and claim-specific evidence promotion.

The contribution is an authorization specification for changes to persistent identity state, not a new entity-matching algorithm or a claim that incorrect identity hypotheses can be eliminated.

It is explicitly presented as a conceptual architecture without implementation or empirical validation.

This application is consistent with the distinction between a diagnostic framework and an operational intervention.

DIO's taxonomy identifies the types of conditions that may undermine reconstruction. The commit-boundary proposal uses selected distinctions to organize authorization requirements before persistent graph changes are accepted.

The application also illustrates the importance of scope.

The manifesto explicitly excludes cybersecurity, authentication and authorization as primary domains of DIO's own digital-identity scope.[2]

The commit-boundary paper does not redefine DIO as a general authentication framework. It applies DIO/ODI distinctions to the specific problem of identity-changing operations in agentic entity resolution.

That is a domain-specific application rather than a change to the core definition.

## 7.3 The Reference Model as a reflexive implementation

One of the most significant emergent findings is that the Reference Model is not merely a technical companion to DIO.

It is an implementation of a central DIO principle within the framework's own publication ecosystem.

DIO is concerned with reconstructing identity from distributed representations.

The Reference Model attempts to make the framework itself reconstructable through:

- Persistent concept identifiers.
- Explicit definitions.
- Source provenance.
- Historical revision notes.
- Typed publication relationships.
- Separated application mappings.
- Versioned archives.
- Machine-readable serializations.

The metadata registry also distinguishes historical platform assertions from current author-verified relationship interpretations.

This is particularly clear in the distribution registry's treatment of the MVDIO copies and the DIO Conceptual Framework's version-scoped relationships.[14]

The registry's role is not to erase all historical inconsistencies automatically.

It records the relationships and their status so that an observer can understand what changed and why.

In DIO terms, this is a mechanism for maintaining referential continuity through explicit historical transformation.

Its effectiveness still depends on observers being able to access and interpret the relevant records.

Nevertheless, the infrastructure makes the intended continuity substantially more inspectable.

## 7.4 The relationship between redundancy and resilience

The ecosystem's distribution model includes multiple representations of the same conceptual corpus.

This creates opportunities for independent access, retrieval and comparison.

However, redundancy alone is not sufficient to establish resilience.

The Hugging Face viewer demonstrates that the same source material can be presented in a way that obscures important document boundaries.

Likewise, a secondary publication copy can preserve the content while carrying a weaker or incorrect metadata relationship.

The value of redundancy therefore depends on whether the representations preserve enough information about their referents, provenance and intended roles.

The ecosystem's most useful form of redundancy is not simply multiple copies of files.

It is the presence of different representations with complementary functions:

- Narrative explanation.
- Archival persistence.
- Semantic identification.
- Publication genealogy.
- Machine-readable retrieval.
- Secondary distribution.

This functional diversity provides more reconstruction options than literal duplication alone.

## 7.5 The role of the author as a persistent identity anchor

The author's identity is represented across the website, repository metadata, citation information and publication records.

The repository includes:

- The author's full name.
- ORCID `0009-0004-0050-0264`.
- A person URI.
- An affiliation statement.
- Citation metadata.

The website provides the professional biography and describes the framework's genesis.

Zenodo records identify the author as creator of the corresponding publications.

These representations connect the author with the framework while keeping the two referents distinct.

This is important.

A reconstruction of Daniel Beránek as a professional SEO specialist and copywriter is not identical to a reconstruction of DIO as a conceptual framework.

The biography provides the relationship between them: DIO is presented as emerging from professional experience and subsequent theoretical articulation.

The archival publications and machine-readable vocabulary then provide representations of the framework that are not dependent solely on the biography.

This is a relatively strong structure for recovering authorship and conceptual origin.

It does not establish that every observer will distinguish the professional persona from the research identity, particularly when encountering the project through commercial or general web-search contexts.

## 7.6 Independent reception and evidence of downstream use

The canonical Zenodo manifesto record exposes view and download statistics, while the Hugging Face dataset exposes its own activity and download counters.

These metrics establish that the resources have been accessed through their respective platforms.

They do not establish conceptual adoption, independent reuse, external validation or successful reconstruction by unrelated observers.

The evidence inspected here also does not establish a substantial body of independent publications applying DIO/ODI.

The absence of such evidence must be interpreted within the scope of the search rather than treated as proof that no external observer has used the framework.

A useful distinction is therefore:

- **Distribution activity:** evidence that resources are available and accessed.
- **Documented reuse:** evidence that another party incorporates or cites the framework.
- **Independent reconstruction:** evidence that an observer can recover its conceptual identity without relying on author-provided interpretation.
- **Independent application:** evidence that the framework is applied to other entities or technical domains by separate operators.

The present snapshot provides evidence of the first category and documented author-developed application mappings.

It does not provide sufficient evidence to quantify the latter categories.

# 8. Synthetic Interpretation

The findings from the conceptual and graph-theoretical analyses converge on an important distinction: a distributed identity can remain recognizable even when its individual representations do not contain the same information or occupy the same temporal state.

This is especially relevant to DIO/ODI because the framework explicitly treats consistency and uniformity as different properties.

The conceptual architecture, the publication lineage and the distribution infrastructure each contribute a different form of continuity.

## 8.1 What supports reconstruction

Four mechanisms are particularly consequential.

**Persistent semantic anchors.**

Stable concept URIs provide identifiable referents across different documents and technical representations.

**Archival and provenance continuity.**

DOIs, versioned releases, source maps and typed relations allow historical and current publications to be distinguished while preserving their relationships.

**Differentiated representation roles.**

The website, archive, vocabulary, repository and secondary distribution channels provide complementary forms of contextual and technical information.

**Explicit treatment of conceptual development.**

Historical entries, source maps, versioned terminology and relation corrections make at least some changes traceable rather than silently replacing earlier states.

Together, these mechanisms support reconstruction at both the conceptual and bibliographic levels.

## 8.2 Where reconstruction remains vulnerable

The main vulnerabilities appear at transitions between representation types.

Examples include:

- A historical-language page encountered as the only available representation.
- A current publication reached only through an older website reference.
- An archival record containing an outdated relationship assertion.
- A machine-readable concept whose historical and current terminology cannot be automatically equated.
- A dataset viewer that obscures the original boundaries of its documents.
- An AI-oriented context file that cannot be acquired through a particular route.

These conditions affect different aspects of reconstruction.

They do not all compromise the same referent.

The multilingual updating problem principally affects the current conceptual detail of language-specific representations.

The publication navigation problem affects recovery of the preferred current work version.

The distribution metadata issue affects the classification of equivalent copies.

The singular/plural vocabulary distinction affects machine-level concept matching.

The Hugging Face viewer affects file and language boundaries.

The inaccessible acquisition routes affect the observer's available evidence.

Treating them as one general category of fragmentation would obscure these differences.

## 8.3 The emerging relationship between conceptual and infrastructural coherence

The graph-theoretical analysis shows that the metadata registry has a recognizable structure with the Reference Model occupying a central outgoing-reference position and the foundational manifesto acting as an important source node.

The conceptual analysis shows that the Reference Model provides an explicit representation of the principal conceptual lineage and its refinements.

These findings reinforce each other.

The metadata structure makes the publication relationships inspectable. The vocabulary makes the meaning of the principal concepts more explicit.

The network does not need to reproduce K6 literally to support the conceptual model.

Its function is to preserve the referential and provenance relationships through which an observer can reconstruct the conceptual system.

The result is a layered architecture:

- Conceptual interdependence.
- Machine-readable semantic relations.
- Publication genealogy.
- Distribution relationships.
- Human-readable narrative context.
- Observer-specific retrieval pathways.

Each layer contributes to a different aspect of reconstruction.

# 9. Conclusion

At the observed point in time, the DIO/ODI ecosystem appears to sustain a recognizable, referentially continuous and meaningfully reconstructable identity across its principal inspected representations.

This conclusion is strongest at the level of the framework's identity and its core conceptual distinctions.

The difference between DIO as applied discipline and ODI as theoretical meta-layer remains visible across the manifesto, conceptual publications and current Reference Vocabulary.

K6, MVDIO, Referential Continuity, Identity-Preserving Variation and the Reconstruction Failure Mode taxonomy provide a recognizable conceptual structure.

The machine-readable layer provides stable concept identifiers, explicit source relationships and a versioned semantic reference.

The publication infrastructure preserves historical work identities and makes their development increasingly traceable.

The website hub provides narrative and multilingual orientation, while the Reference Model and its repository provide a more structured account of current concepts, publications and applications.

The multilingual archive preserves sixteen language projections of the foundational manifesto, while the living Czech and English editions make later conceptual development accessible.

These representations are not synchronized to the same level of detail, and their individual distribution interfaces do not always preserve the intended structure.

That limitation is particularly relevant when reconstructing the latest conceptual state or the precise relationship between versions and external distribution copies.

The graph analysis further demonstrates that the declared publication metadata has a meaningful, differentiated structure.

Its 21 typed directed relationships among 17 identifiers show a recognizable publication lineage, a central Reference Model aggregation layer and distinct distribution relationships.

This is not a measurement of the entire public web, but it provides an inspectable structural basis for understanding how the current corpus is organized.

The principal weaknesses remain concentrated around particular transitions:

- Between historical and current editions.
- Between language projections.
- Between archival metadata and the operational registry.
- Between source documents and platform-generated representations.
- Between the author's professional identity and the conceptual identity of the framework.

These transitions can impair specific reconstruction tasks, but the available evidence does not indicate that they collectively destroy the referential continuity of DIO/ODI.

The strongest interpretation is therefore that the ecosystem exhibits **referential continuity with uneven representational currency**.

Its resilience does not depend on every representation being identical or every update being synchronized.

It depends on the availability of sufficient anchors and relationships through which historical and current representations can be recognized as related states of the same evolving conceptual framework.

The most significant development is that the framework's own semantic and provenance principles are increasingly reflected in its implementation.

The Reference Model, metadata registry, source map and application mappings make the relationships between concepts, publications and versions more explicit.

At the same time, the distinction between a documented safeguard and a demonstrated outcome remains important. Persistent identifiers do not guarantee correct interpretation. Provenance metadata does not establish cryptographic authenticity. Multiple distribution channels do not automatically compensate for structural loss in a particular interface.

The ecosystem should therefore be understood neither as a perfectly synchronized architecture nor as a fragmented collection of unrelated artifacts.

It is a developing distributed identity system whose principal conceptual referents remain recoverable through several complementary pathways, while certain detailed reconstructions still depend on temporal context, careful interpretation and access to the appropriate representation.

In DIO/ODI terms, the relevant achievement is not permanent completeness.

It is the maintenance of sufficient referential continuity and semantic coherence for an observer to reconstruct the framework meaningfully while its representations continue to change.
